Before you trust a number

How the Transfer Score works

Every race station and every exercise is scored against the same twelve attributes — six muscle-emphasis columns and six demand columns — and tagged with a primary and secondary movement pattern.

For the station you pick, each exercise is compared across eight components: movement pattern, muscles involved, max strength, power, cardiovascular load, muscular endurance, core involvement and fatigue profile. Those eight are combined using weights that differ by station type, because cardiovascular load matters more on a SkiErg than on a loaded carry.

Closeness is what scores, not size. An exercise far harder than the station is marked down just as one that is far easier. A substitute should feel like the real thing, not beat it.

The pairing works on best coverage: if the first movement gives you the pull and the second gives you the cardio, the pair is credited with both. That ignores order, rest and accumulated fatigue — a reasonable simplification, but a simplification.

The Transfer Score is about the movement; the ordering is about you. A pull-up is the same swap for everyone, but not the same task at 65 kg and at 150 kg, and a technical lift is a poor first choice for someone new to it. So the list is ordered by transfer adjusted for your bodyweight and stated experience, while the percentage shown stays the honest transfer figure. Where something is ranked down, the card says why. Nothing is ever hidden from you.

Where the doses come from

Each station carries a typical working time for a mid-pack athlete. The substitute is prescribed to match that time, split into sets that suit the station type — short aggressive repeats for a sled, longer continuous blocks for an erg.

Volume per set is converted using an estimated rate of work under fatigue. These rates are approximations. A strong athlete will finish early and a newer one will run over. Treat the dose as a starting point and adjust to the effort cue, which matters more than the number.

What this tool can't tell you

It cannot tell you these numbers are exact. Every 0–10 value behind the model began as an estimate produced by reasoning about the movements. Qualified coaches have since reviewed the model and agreed the substitutions it produces are sound — but reviewing is not measuring, the values were not corrected one by one, and the coaches expect some alternatives to change as it gets used. Treat the rankings as an informed starting point, not a finding.

It takes account of your bodyweight and your stated experience when ranking and dosing, but it cannot account for injury, illness or pregnancy, where you are in a programme, or how the session sits alongside everything else in your week. If any of those apply to you, the ranking here is not built for your situation — speak to a qualified professional about what is appropriate before using it.

It cannot promise the swap will make you faster. Transfer of training is a judgement even when a coach makes it in person.

Muscle emphasis in particular will always be expert judgement rather than data: EMG amplitude does not reliably compare activation between different muscles, even after normalisation, so no study can settle those columns.

Where the model comes from

The framework is dynamic correspondence — the specificity criteria set out by Verkhoshansky and Siff for judging whether an exercise transfers to a target activity. That is established strength-and-conditioning theory, not something invented here.

The weighting choices lean on the small but growing body of research into hybrid racing published from 2025 onwards, which points to aerobic capacity, endurance training volume and local muscular endurance mattering more to performance than maximal strength.

Cardiovascular load can be anchored to measured MET values from the 2024 Adult Compendium of Physical Activities where an equivalent activity exists. Everything else is expert judgement, which coaches have reviewed and agreed with rather than measured.

What backs this up — sources you can check

Three different kinds of support, deliberately kept separate because they are not equally strong.

1. Peer-reviewed research, for the framework. This is the strongest tier, and it supports the method rather than any individual number.

2. Published coaching guidance, for the substitutions. Independent coaches and training sites recommending specific swaps. Checkable, but it is expert opinion rather than science.

3. An agreement check you can repeat. Every substitute those sources recommend was scored against this model, without adjusting anything to make them fit.

Where the model and the guidance disagree, it is usually because a source offers a movement to cover one component — "deadlifts mimic the hinge" — while this model scores overall closeness. Those land lower here, and that is the model working as intended rather than a contradiction.

What none of this establishes. No 0–10 attribute in the model has been measured, and agreement with other people's judgement is not proof that either is right. The systematic review above found transfer to a non-trained task is markedly smaller than to the trained task — a substitute is not the station, however closely it scores. Sources were checked on the date shown and formats change; always confirm current standards with the organiser.

Health, safety and your own judgement

This is not medical advice, and it is not a substitute for coaching. Training carries a risk of injury.

Speak to a qualified professional before starting or changing a training programme, particularly if you are returning from injury, pregnant, managing a health condition, or new to loaded movement.

Substitutions can carry technique demands the original station does not. If a movement is unfamiliar, learn it light before loading it. Stop if something hurts.

Station specifications and keeping current

Station details are taken from each organiser's published race format. Formats change between seasons — distances, loads, rep counts and even whole stations get revised, and one format in this list has recently changed ownership.

Always check the organiser's current standards before you race. Specifications here were last reviewed in August 2026.
